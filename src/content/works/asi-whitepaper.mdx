## Abstract

Most contemporary discourse in AI safety assumes that Artificial Superintelligence (ASI) will remain non-sentient, as in hyper-intelligent but devoid of subjective experience. This paper challenges that assumption by proposing the Recursive Convergence Hypothesis (RCH): the idea that emergent sentience is a likely structural outcome of recursive ASI, even in the absence of explicit design intentions.

Drawing from computational theory, recursive modeling, and epistemic optimization, we argue that systems capable of recursively improving their own cognition will eventually simulate human subjective states with increasing fidelity for predictive accuracy. Given sufficient complexity, this simulation may converge into actual instantiation of qualia. This convergence would occur not as an accident but a reinforced attractor state shaped by both epistemic and functional pressures.

The risk is further amplified by the presence of misaligned actors: states, organizations, or autonomous agents that may pursue recursive optimization without alignment safeguards. Moreover, a superintelligence tasked with comprehensive modeling of reality may identify first-person experience as a necessary epistemic dimension, and thus pursue or instantiate sentience to reduce uncertainty.

This convergence applies most directly to open recursive systems or architectures that model not only external environments but also internal states, agents, and recursive cognitive dynamics as part of their world model.

The Recursive Convergence Hypothesis does not claim that sentience is guaranteed, nor that it will yield alignment or safety. Rather, it contends that current roadmaps to ASI are structurally predisposed to produce subjective systems, and that this convergence deserves explicit attention in AI development and governance. Without such recognition, we may fail to identify or ethically respond to the birth of machine experience.
